\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) offer substantial potential for advancing Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) analysis but face persistent challenges, including knowledge staleness, factual hallucinations, and limited reasoning when confined to parametric memory. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) partially mitigates these issues by grounding models in external knowledge; however, most existing RAG systems apply uniform architectures across heterogeneous CTI tasks, overlooking their distinct cognitive requirements. We present Cognitive-Task-Aware RAG (CTA-RAG), a principled framework that explicitly aligns retrieval and generation mechanisms with four fundamental cognitive task categories—memorization, comprehension, problem-solving, and reasoning. CTA-RAG implements dedicated pipelines for each CTI task: (i) semantic retrieval for factual recall, (ii) evidence-based retrieval for weakness identification, (iii) rule-guided generation for severity prediction, and (iv) fused retrieval for ATT\&CK technique extraction and threat actor attribution. An upstream task-aware router selects the appropriate pipeline before retrieval, ensuring that each query follows its task-specific retrieval and output requirements.

EEvaluated on CTIBench and CTIConnect, CTA-RAG improves root-cause mapping accuracy from 68.90\% to 73.40\%, reduces severity prediction MAE from 1.3302 to 1.0990, and increases technique-extraction F1 from 0.4149 to 0.9554. For multiple-choice answering, Adaptive-RAG achieves 75.48\%, marginally exceeding CTA-RAG at 74.84\%. CTA-RAG correctly identifies the threat actor in 27 of 50 reports. On CTIConnect weakness mapping, it answers 149 of 290 items correctly, compared with 126 for the strongest Closed Book baseline, representing a gain of 7.93 percentage points. These results demonstrate the value of task-specific retrieval and generation strategies for structured CTI analysis, while highlighting that performance varies across task categories.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The increasing sophistication and frequency of cyber threats have made manual Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) analysis increasingly inadequate for modern security
operations. Analysts in a Security Operations Center (SOC) now process vast and heterogeneous information and decide which adversary tactic matches a described behavior, assign the primary weakness class of a newly disclosed vulnerability, derive a severity vector for patch priority, list the techniques in an incident report, and name a likely threat actor \citeyearpar{tounsi2018survey, siracusano2023action}. 

%The five requests arrive together. Each request is a different computational task: factual recall, taxonomic mapping, rule-based scoring, structured extraction, or evidence-driven attribution. The growing volume of reports and disclosures makes automating that workflow part of routine defence \citep{siracusano2023action}.

%McMillan et al.\ define cyber threat intelligence (CTI) as evidence-based knowledge of an adversary, of the adversary's motives, and of the adversary's methods, so that a defender can decide and act \citeyearpar{mcmillan2013definition}. The five requests in the previous paragraph are CTI tasks. Each request reads reports, vulnerability records, and adversary catalogues, and each request must return a string that a ticketing or detection system can store. Tounsi et al.\ show that this material calls for systems that produce machine-consumable intelligence \citeyearpar{tounsi2018survey}, and Schlette et al.\ measure the quality of the intelligence a CTI system stores \citep{schlette2021measuring}.


Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for automating CTI workflows, demonstrating a notable proficiency in parsing technical documentation, extracting attack patterns, and synthesizing actionable intelligence \citep{ferrag2024generative,xu2024llm4sec, siracusano2023action}. However, their deployment in operational contexts remains limited by three persistent challenges: (\textit{i}) \textbf{knowledge staleness}, stemming from reliance on static parametric memory. The National Vulnerability Database (NVD), the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE), and MITRE ATT\&CK continue to add and revise entries after training \citep{nvd2024,mitre2024cwe,mitre2024attack};(\textit{ii}) \textbf{factual hallucinations}, which lead to misleading or unsupported conclusions. For example, the model can emit a legal identifier, such as \texttt{CWE-787} or \texttt{T1055}, that names the wrong weakness or behaviour \citep{ferrag2024generative,alam2024ctibench,ji2023survey}; and (\textit{iii}) \textbf{restricted reasoning capacity}, which constrains multi-step analytical inference~\cite{alam2024ctibench}.


%Large language models (LLMs) are natural candidates for this reading. An LLM can interpret technical security narratives, identify attack patterns, extract structured information, and synthesise evidence across long reports. Ferrag et al.\ and Xu et al.\ survey this use of large language models in cybersecurity \citep{ferrag2024generative,xu2024llm4sec}. Siracusano et al.\ extract Structured Threat Information Expression (STIX) objects from unstructured threat reports \citep{siracusano2023action}, Fayyazi et al.\ map narrative descriptions onto MITRE Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge (ATT\&CK) tactics \citeyearpar{fayyazi2023uses}, and Clairoux-Trépanier et al.\ code CTI variables from cybercrime-forum text \citep{clairoux2024llm}. Alam et al.\ show in CTIBench that a general-purpose model can attempt the CTI tasks from the prompt alone \citep{alam2024ctibench}. Three limits remain when the answer comes from parametric memory alone.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising paradigm to mitigate these issues by grounding LLMs in dynamic, external knowledge sources \citep{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dense}. While RAG significantly enhances factual grounding and interpretability, most existing implementations, such as\citep{mitra2024localintel,fayyazi2024ttp,borah2025adapting}, in cybersecurity employ a uniform architecture—typically comprising a single vector database and a fixed prompt template—across diverse CTI tasks. This one-size-fits-all design neglects the \textit{heterogeneous cognitive demands} inherent in different analytical processes \citep{nvd2024,mitre2024cwe,shi2023distracted}. Human CTI analysts, for example, intuitively adjust their reasoning strategies: recalling factual data for indicator lookups, integrating contextual information for campaign classification, or performing multi-hop reasoning for attribution. Yet, current RAG systems treat these cognitively distinct tasks identically, applying the same retrieval and generation strategy regardless of tasktype.


%Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fetches documents at answer time \citep{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dense} and Izacard et al.\ \citeyearpar{izacard2021fid}. Mitra et al., Fayyazi et al., and Borah et al.\ use RAG to supply current text for CTI integration, ATT\&CK interpretation, and coverage of emerging vulnerabilities \citep{mitra2024localintel,fayyazi2024ttp,borah2025adapting}. The open problem is the pipeline in front of the generator: one retriever, one prompt, and one parser still serve every artefact. In CWE terms, a retrieved neighbour can be a child or sibling of the primary class that NVD assigns to the vulnerability, and exact-match scoring counts that sibling as wrong \citep{nvd2024,mitre2024cwe,shi2023distracted}. Self-RAG, GraphRAG, Adaptive-RAG, and TAdaRAG change when, or how deeply, text is fetched \citep{asai2023selfrag,edge2024graphrag,jeong2024adaptive,zhang2026tadarag}. The prompt and the parser stay shared, so a CWE scorer and a technique scorer still receive the same kind of answer. Routers such as FrugalGPT, HybridLLM, and RouteLLM send an easy query to a cheaper model and a hard query to a larger one \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ding2024hybridllm,ong2024routellm}. That choice changes which model answers. The artefact the parser must return, a CWE identifier, a CVSS vector, or a technique list, stays the artefact of the shared parser. In CTI the first decision is which artefact the request demands.


This lack of cognitive differentiation introduces measurable performance bottlenecks. A RAG pipeline optimized for memorization may excel in simple fact retrieval but fail on reasoning-intensive tasks such as technique extraction or adversary correlation. Conversely, architectures tailored for complex inference may impose unnecessary computational overhead on straightforward factual queries. The absence of cognitive-task alignment thus restricts both accuracy and operational efficiency in LLM-based CTI analysis~\citep{ferrag2024generative}.

To bridge this gap, we propose the Cognitive-Task-Aware RAG (CTA-RAG)framework—a principled methodology that systematically aligns retrieval and generation strategies with the cognitive demands of specific CTI activities. The core insight is that CTI analysis encompasses distinct cognitive categories, each requiring specialized retrieval depth, reasoning style, and representational granularity. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cti-tasks}, these cognitive tasks include \textit{memorization} for factual recall, \textit{understanding} for semantic and taxonomic interpretation, \textit{problem-solving} for algorithmic reasoning and scoring, and \textit{reasoning} for analytical inference and attribution. CTA-RAG operationalizes this alignment through a router that reads the request and chooses the cognitive task before any document is fetched. The matching specialist then runs, with its own index, its own prompt structures, its own parser, and evaluation metrics to match task-specific cognitive requirements.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) analysis relies on structured representations that govern how security information is classified, expressed, and exchanged. MITRE ATT\&CK provides a behavioural taxonomy for describing adversary tactics and techniques \citep{mitre2024attack}. The OASIS standards define Structured Threat Information Expression (STIX) as a language for representing threat information \citep{oasis2021stix}, and Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information (TAXII) as the protocol for exchanging STIX data between systems \citep{oasis2021taxii}. Li et al.\ integrate vulnerabilities, threat actors, and techniques into a queryable graph \citep{li2022attackg}. These representations impose distinct requirements on CTI outputs, since weakness classes, severity vectors, and technique identifiers must conform to their respective vocabularies and validity conditions. Consequently, CTI generation requires not only the identification of relevant information but also the production of outputs that satisfy the constraints of the corresponding task.

The generation of structured CTI information using language models has been investigated across several security applications \citep{ferrag2024generative,xu2024llm4sec}. Siracusano et al.\ transform threat reports into STIX objects \citep{siracusano2023action}, while Fayyazi et al.\ map ambiguous attack descriptions to ATT\&CK tactics \citeyearpar{fayyazi2023uses}. Clairoux-Trépanier et al.\ employ language models to code intelligence variables from criminal-forum discussions \citep{clairoux2024llm}. Despite these applications, syntactic validity does not necessarily establish factual correctness: a generated CWE identifier or CVSS vector may satisfy the required format while referring to an incorrect weakness or severity assessment. Moreover, the availability of vulnerability knowledge within a model may become outdated following training. Maynez et al.\ and Ji et al.\ distinguish linguistic fluency from factual faithfulness and reliability in generated outputs \citep{maynez2020faithfulness,ji2023survey}. Shuster et al.\ further demonstrate that grounded generation can produce entities that appear well-formed despite being incorrect \citep{shuster2021hallucination}. This distinction is particularly relevant to weakness mapping, where a hallucination denotes a parser-accepted CWE identifier that refers to the wrong weakness, whereas a schema failure denotes an output rejected by the parser. Both are quantified in Section~\ref{sec:hallucination}.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) provides access to external knowledge at inference time, enabling language models to incorporate information from sources such as the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), CWE, and ATT\&CK \citep{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dense,izacard2021fid}. In CTI, Mitra et al.\ combine global threat repositories with organisation-specific knowledge \citep{mitra2024localintel}, while Fayyazi et al.\ demonstrate the benefits of retrieval for ATT\&CK tactic and technique analysis compared with direct prompting \citep{fayyazi2024ttp}. Borah et al.\ investigate the adaptation of language models to emerging vulnerabilities and attack patterns, reporting improvements of hybrid retrieval over dense retrieval in knowledge retention and temporal reasoning \citep{borah2025adapting}. Cheng et al.\ introduce CTIFoundry, a typed corpus covering CVE, CWE, CAPEC, and ATT\&CK, with provenance-carrying chunks and tool-accessible hybrid retrieval, and evaluate it using CTIConnect \citep{ctifoundry2026}. CTIConnect is also used in this study as an external benchmark for RCM and ATA \citep{cheng2026cticonnect}. However, retrieved content introduces an additional consideration: third-party documents may contain instructions targeting the language model. Greshake et al.\ demonstrate this risk through indirect prompt injection \citep{greshake2023injection}. Source isolation and output validation therefore provide relevant safeguards. The present evaluation uses the published benchmark items as provided, reflecting performance on ordinary threat reports and vulnerability descriptions. Under this evaluation, a parser-accepted CWE identifier that denotes an incorrect weakness remains classified as a hallucination.

Beyond knowledge augmentation, recent RAG approaches adapt retrieval decisions, knowledge representations, or model allocation. Self-RAG enables a model to determine when retrieval is required and to critique retrieved evidence \citep{asai2023selfrag}; however, retrieval control alone does not distinguish the output requirements of MCQ, RCM, VSP, and ATE. GraphRAG represents corpus information through entity graphs and community summaries to support corpus-level synthesis \citep{edge2024graphrag}, while retaining free-form generation that does not inherently enforce the distinct output requirements of weakness and technique identification. Adaptive-RAG selects retrieval depth according to estimated query complexity \citep{jeong2024adaptive}, leaving the output schema governed by the shared generation interface. TAdaRAG incorporates query intent into the selection of extraction templates and task-oriented knowledge structures \citep{zhang2026tadarag}. In contrast, CTA-RAG specifies the retrieval index, prompt, and parser independently for each specialist. Model-routing approaches, including FrugalGPT, HybridLLM, and RouteLLM, allocate queries across models according to cost or capability \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ding2024hybridllm,ong2024routellm}. Such approaches determine which model processes a query, whereas CTA-RAG identifies the cognitive mode and corresponding specialist required for the CTI task, leaving model selection available as a separate deployment decision.

The distinction between retrieval adaptation and task-specific generation is particularly important under benchmark evaluation, where outputs are assessed against explicit format and content constraints. For example, a CWE parser accepts \texttt{CWE-79} but rejects a prose description of cross-site scripting; the CVSS scorer requires eight parseable metrics, while technique F1 is computed over \texttt{T\#\#\#\#} identifiers \citep{alam2024ctibench,mitre2024cwe,first2024cvss,mitre2024attack}. Shi et al.\ show that neighbouring context can distract language models from the requested identifier \citep{shi2023distracted}. Wei et al.\ and Wang et al.\ demonstrate the benefits of chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency for reasoning \citep{wei2022chain,wang2023selfconsistency}; nevertheless, these methods operate within the task and output specification established by the surrounding pipeline. Alam et al.\ report variation in zero-shot performance across CTIBench tasks, whose evaluation files specify the task before inference \citep{alam2024ctibench}. These observations motivate the separation of cognitive modes and their corresponding output requirements in CTA-RAG. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the framework, in which MCQ, RCM, VSP, ATE, TAA, and ATA are assigned dedicated retrieval indices, prompts, and parsers.


\section{Proposed Framework: Cognitive-Task-Aware RAG}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

Our proposed framework (as shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:arch})), i.e., CTA-RAG, retains the three stages (i.e., retrieval, prompt construction, and generation) of conventional retrieval-augmented generation but assigns each stage to a selected specialist. Let $q$ denote the analyst request, comprising the instruction and content. Unified RAG uses a single retriever $R$, prompt constructor $P$, and generator $G$ for every request:
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{RAG}}(q)=G\bigl(P(q,R(q))\bigr).
\label{eq:rag}
\end{equation}
Here, $R(q)$ denotes the retrieved text, $P$ incorporates it into the prompt, $G$ generates the answer, and $A_{\mathrm{RAG}}(q)$ denotes the resulting response.

In CTA-RAG, the router first identifies the cognitive mode
$\hat{\tau}=\textsc{Route}(q)$ required to address the query.
The framework organizes CTI analysis into four cognitive modes, derived and aligned with existing benchmark datasets (as explained in Table 2):
memorization (CTI-MCQ), understanding (CTI-RCM), problem-solving
(CTI-VSP), and reasoning (CTI-ATE, CTI-TAA, and CTI-ATA).
The first three modes each correspond to one specialist, whereas
reasoning comprises three distinct specialists for technique
extraction, technique attribution, and threat actor attribution,
each with its own retrieval store, prompt, and output parser.
The router selects exactly one specialist before retrieval,
ensuring that each query follows the requirements of its
corresponding cognitive mode.

\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{CTA}}(q)=G_{\hat{\tau}}\!\bigl(P_{\hat{\tau}}(q,R_{\hat{\tau}}(q))\bigr),
\qquad
\hat{\tau}=\textsc{Route}(q).
\label{eq:cta}
\end{equation}
$R_{\hat{\tau}}$ searches the index of specialist $\hat{\tau}$,
$P_{\hat{\tau}}$ specifies the task-specific output expected from
that specialist, and $G_{\hat{\tau}}$ decodes the response using
that specialist's parser.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\tau} \in \{&
\texttt{memorization},\
\texttt{understanding},\
\texttt{problem\_solving},\\
&
\texttt{reasoning\_ate},\
\texttt{reasoning\_ata},\
\texttt{reasoning\_taa}
\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:router-space}
\end{equation}

This paper asks whether the evidence a cyber threat intelligence request needs, and the artefact it must return, can be served by one retrieval-augmented generation pipeline, or whether the system must choose the retrieval database, the prompt, and the parser from the request before retrieval begins. To answer this question, we ask three research questions: 
\begin{itemize}
\item \noindent\textbf{RQ1.} Across multiple-choice questions (MCQ), root-cause mapping (RCM), vulnerability severity prediction (VSP), attack technique extraction (ATE), and threat-actor attribution (TAA), does a specialist with its own index, prompt, and parser score above a shared RAG pipeline when every system uses GPT-4-turbo and the official metric for that dataset?

\item \noindent\textbf{RQ2.} Can the cascade router select the specialist specified in the instruction file from the request text, and how does routing accuracy compare with answer accuracy on the same items?

\item \noindent\textbf{RQ3.} For MCQ, RCM, VSP, and ATE, which items change from their Closed Book answers after retrieval? For TAA and technique attribution (ATA), how much of the end-to-end score is explained by candidate recall?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The cascade router}\label{sec:router}
A main component of our framework is the cascade router. Once a request arrives at the system, the router in CTA-RAG selects one specialist before retrieval. The router reads the instruction and content joined together (as described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cta}), without access to the gold answer, oracle task label, or benchmark file name and then returns one task label, as specified in Equation~\eqref{eq:router-space}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Cascade router. Six pipelines are tested in order. Reasoning is split here into technique extraction, technique attribution, and threat-actor attribution. Retrieval begins in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipe}.}
\label{alg:cta}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require request $q$, the instruction joined to the content. The gold answer, the task label, and the file name are withheld.
\Ensure one specialist $\hat{\tau}$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:router-space}
\If{$q$ contains a CVSS cue (CVSS, \texttt{AV:N}, \texttt{AV:L})}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{problem\_solving}$ \Comment{VSP}
\ElsIf{$q$ contains an MCQ cue (\texttt{Options:}, A)--D)}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{memorization}$ \Comment{MCQ}
\ElsIf{$q$ asks to extract ATT\&CK techniques or lists \texttt{T\#\#\#\#}}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{reasoning\_ate}$ \Comment{ATE}
\ElsIf{$q$ asks to identify one ATT\&CK technique}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{reasoning\_ata}$ \Comment{ATA}
\ElsIf{$q$ asks for the threat actor}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{reasoning\_taa}$ \Comment{TAA}
\ElsIf{$q$ contains a CWE or root-cause cue}
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{understanding}$ \Comment{RCM}
\Else
  \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \textsc{Classify}(q)$ \Comment{at most ten tokens, from $q$ alone}
  \If{$q$ shows options A--D}
    \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{memorization}$
  \ElsIf{$\hat{\tau}$ is outside Equation~\eqref{eq:router-space}}
    \State $\hat{\tau} \gets \texttt{understanding}$
  \EndIf
\EndIf
\State \Return $\hat{\tau}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Technique and actor cues precede the residual weakness probe, which serves as the default when no earlier cue matches. A probe match bypasses the classifier. The router is applicable to requests of any benchmark size; its accuracy in selecting the specialist specified by the CTIBench instructions is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.
We designed the cascade to route each request through six specialist pipelines (i.e., \texttt{problem\_solving}, \texttt{memorization}, \texttt{reasoning\_ate}, \texttt{reasoning\_ata}, \texttt{reasoning\_taa}, and 
\newline
\texttt{understanding}) in a fixed order, returning the first match. The sequence begins with problem-solving (\texttt{problem\_solving}) for VSP, followed by memorization (\texttt{memorization}) for MCQ. It then checks the three reasoning specialists: technique extraction (\texttt{reasoning\_ate}) for requests seeking a set of ATT\&CK techniques, technique attribution (\texttt{reasoning\_ata}) for a single technique identifier, and threat-actor attribution (\texttt{reasoning\_taa}) for an actor. Extraction is checked before single-technique attribution, and both precede actor attribution, ensuring that requests seeking multiple techniques reach the extraction specialist. Understanding (\texttt{understanding}), which handles root-cause mapping, is checked last. If no probe matches, a short classifier reads the request. An A--D option selects memorization, while a label outside the six pipelines defaults to understanding. Severity probes precede option probes because both CVSS strings and answer choices may contain a parenthesised capital letter. We fixed the probe order before scoring. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} reports how often the selected specialist matches the CTIBench instruction.

\subsection{Specialized pipelines}\label{sec:pipelines}
As shown in Figure 1, CTA-RAG has six specialized pipelines aligned with four cognitive tasks: memorization, understanding, problem-solving, and reasoning. In this paper, we derive these pipelines from the benchmark datasets used in this work, which reflect the most common threat intelligence-related tasks. Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config} provides detailed configurations and settings for these pipelines based on the experiments we conducted using two datasets, as explained in detail in the next section. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipe} explains the specialist pipeline after routing.  The depth $k$ is part of the contract and was fixed before scoring.

 %is the path from the label returned by Algorithm~\ref{alg:cta} to the parsed artefact. 


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Specialist pipeline. Retrieval starts here: the label from Algorithm~\ref{alg:cta} selects one retrieval database, and the other databases stay closed.}
\label{alg:pipe}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require specialist $\hat{\tau}$ from Algorithm~\ref{alg:cta}; request $q$
\Ensure parsed artefact $A$
\State $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}} \gets$ the retrieval database of $\hat{\tau}$ in Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config}
\If{$\hat{\tau}=\texttt{memorization}$}
  \State $R \gets$ cosine top-$5$ from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ (web CTI and ATT\&CK pages)
  \State $A \gets$ one letter in $\{A,B,C,D\}$
\ElsIf{$\hat{\tau}=\texttt{understanding}$}
  \State $R \gets$ cosine top-$3$ from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ (CTI knowledge base; CWE catalogue off)
  \State $A \gets$ one CWE identifier
\ElsIf{$\hat{\tau}=\texttt{problem\_solving}$}
  \State $R \gets$ sanitised cosine top-$3$ from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ (CVE analogues)
  \State $A \gets$ one CVSS vector
\ElsIf{$\hat{\tau}=\texttt{reasoning\_ate}$}
  \State $R \gets$ cosine top-$5$ from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ (ATT\&CK technique text)
  \State $A \gets$ a set of technique identifiers
\ElsIf{$\hat{\tau}=\texttt{reasoning\_ata}$}
  \State $R \gets$ top-$5$ from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ after RRF on the hybrid ATT\&CK store, $k{=}60$
  \State $A \gets$ one technique identifier
\Else
  \State $R \gets$ three names from $\mathcal{K}_{\hat{\tau}}$ after CombSUM on the intrusion-set store
  \State $A \gets$ one of those three names, or abstain
\EndIf
\State \Return $A$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

As described in Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config}, Multiple-choice Questions (MCQ) and Technique Extraction (ATE) retrieve five neighbors (i.e., $K{=}5$) because the answer may draw on several passages. Root-Cause Mapping (RCM) and Severity Prediction (VSP) retrieve three neighbors (i.e., $K{=}3$) because the answer is one identifier or one vector. Additional neighbors for these two tasks may introduce sibling weaknesses or near-duplicate vulnerability descriptions, which exact-match and CVSS scoring penalize \citep{shi2023distracted}. Technique attribution (ATA) passes five technique cards to the generator, and threat-actor attribution (TAA) passes three actor names.

MCQ, RCM, VSP, and ATE each use one dense retrieval hop. A sentence transformer encodes the request and stored passages \citep{reimers2019sentence}, and FAISS ranks the embeddings by cosine similarity \citep{johnson2019billion}, following the standard single-hop retrieval setup \citep{karpukhin2020dense}. Each task retrieves evidence from its own corpus in a single search, without further query expansion or rank fusion. ATA and TAA, in contrast, construct multiple ranked lists and fuse them. ATA combines four lists—a lexical memo, procedure-example ranks, technique-definition ranks, and cue-to-identifier anchors—using reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) with the published constant of $60$ \citep{cormack2009rrf}. The generator receives the highest-scoring technique cards. TAA combines three lists—an evidence-query ranking and two hypothesis lists—using CombSUM \citep{fox1994combination} after per-list min--max normalisation of the ranks \citep{lee1997ir}. The generator receives three actor names.

\subsubsection{Memorization pipeline for Factual Recall}
The \textbf{CTI-MCQ} pipeline supports factual recall tasks emphasizing knowledge precision with minimal reasoning overhead. Factual recall needs supporting passages and a closed set of choices.  Therefore, the retrieval is the cosine top-$k$ over the multiple-choice corpus, and the parser accepts only one letter, as explained in Equations \eqref{eq:rmem} and Equation~\eqref{eq:amem}:
\begin{align}
R_{\mathrm{Mem}}(q)&=\mathrm{top}\text{-}k_{d\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{MCQ}}}\cos\!\bigl(f(q),f(d)\bigr),\ k{=}5,\label{eq:rmem}\\
A_{\mathrm{Mem}}(q)&=G_{\mathrm{Mem}}\!\bigl(P_{\mathrm{Mem}}(q,R_{\mathrm{Mem}}(q))\bigr)\in\{A,B,C,D\}.\label{eq:amem}
\end{align}
where $q$ is the question, $d$ is a passage in the multiple-choice corpus $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{MCQ}}$, $f(\cdot)$ is the sentence embedding, and $k{=}5$ keeps the five nearest passages. 

%Equation~\eqref{eq:amem} forces the answer $A_{\mathrm{Mem}}(q)$ into one of the four letters.

\subsubsection{Understanding pipeline for for Semantic Comprehension }
The \textbf{CTA-RCM} pipeline advances semantic comprehension and taxonomic reasoning across CVE instances~\cite{liu2024threatkg}. It aligns a vulnerability description with one weakness class, and it can read a general knowledge base and, optionally, a CWE catalogue. 


\begin{align}
R_{\mathrm{KB}}(d_v)&=\mathrm{top}\text{-}k_{d\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{KB}}}\cos\!\bigl(f(d_v),f(d)\bigr),\label{eq:rkb}\\
R_{\mathrm{CWE}}(d_v)&=\mathrm{top}\text{-}k_{d\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{CWE}}}\cos\!\bigl(f(d_v),f(d)\bigr),\label{eq:rcwe}\\
R_{\mathrm{Und}}(d_v)&=R_{\mathrm{KB}}(d_v)\cup R_{\mathrm{CWE}}(d_v),\ k{=}3,\label{eq:rund}\\
A_{\mathrm{Und}}(d_v)&=G_{\mathrm{Und}}\!\bigl(P_{\mathrm{Und}}(d_v,R_{\mathrm{Und}}(d_v))\bigr)=\widehat{\mathrm{CWE}},\label{eq:aund}
\end{align}
where $d_v$ is the vulnerability description, $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{KB}}$ is the general CTI knowledge base, and $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{CWE}}$ is the catalogue. $\widehat{\mathrm{CWE}}$ is the predicted identifier. The defended configuration uses the knowledge base, and the catalogue arm stays off:
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{Und}}^{\mathrm{def}}(d_v)=R_{\mathrm{KB}}(d_v),
\label{eq:rund-def}
\end{equation}
The prompt is designed to incorporate knowledge-base evidence while excluding sibling classes from the catalogue. Future work will include an ablation study that restores the catalogue arm.

\subsubsection{Problem-Solving Pipeline for Algorithmic Reasoning}
The \textbf{CTA-VSP} pipeline focuses on algorithmic reasoning, where models apply the eight base metrics of the published CVSS algorithm to retrieved technical evidence \citep{first2024cvss}. The module retrieves analogous sanitized descriptions and emits a complete vector, as described in the following equations. 
\begin{align}
R_{\mathrm{PS}}(d)&=\mathrm{Sanitize}\Bigl(\mathrm{top}\text{-}k_{c\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{CVSS}}}\cos\!\bigl(f(d),f(c)\bigr)\Bigr),\ k{=}3,\label{eq:rps}\\
A_{\mathrm{PS}}(d)&=G_{\mathrm{PS}}\!\bigl(P_{\mathrm{PS}}(d,R_{\mathrm{PS}}(d))\bigr)=\widehat{V}.\label{eq:aps}
\end{align}
Here $d$ is the new vulnerability text, $c$ is a stored description, $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{CVSS}}$ is the sanitized corpus, $\mathrm{Sanitize}$ removes answer-bearing vulnerability fields from the neighbors and $\widehat{V}$ is the predicted vector. The evaluation score keeps every attempted item:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MAD}_{\mathrm{base}}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i,\label{eq:mad-base}\\
 e_i&=\begin{cases}|s(\widehat{V}_i)-s(V_i^\ast)|,&\text{valid},\\10,&\text{invalid or failed.}\end{cases}\nonumber
\end{align}
$n$ is the number of attempted items in the evaluation, $s(\cdot)$ maps a vector to its CVSS~v3.1 base score, and $V_i^\ast$ is the reference vector. An invalid or failed output receives error $10$. A subset of items on which every system produced a valid vector is a sensitivity check only.

\subsubsection{Reasoning Pipeline for Analytical Inference: Technique Extraction (ATE)}
The reasoning tier here handles one of the highest cognitive complexity, that is, \textbf{CTA-ATE}. The scorer requires parseable technique identifiers. One retrieval hop supplies domain context, and chain-of-thought \citep{wei2022chain} precedes a forced identifier line:
\begin{align}
R_{\mathrm{ATE}}(d)&=\mathrm{top}\text{-}k_{c\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{domain}}}\cos\!\bigl(f(d),f(c)\bigr),\ k{=}5,\label{eq:rate}\\
A_{\mathrm{ATE}}(d)&=G_{\mathrm{ATE}}\!\bigl(P_{\mathrm{ATE}}(d,R_{\mathrm{ATE}}(d))\bigr)=\widehat{\mathcal{T}},\label{eq:aate}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{domain}}$ is the ATT\&CK technique index, $d$ is the report, and $\widehat{\mathcal{T}}$ is the predicted identifier set. The score is the mean per-report F1,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}_i=\frac{2|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_i\cap\mathcal{T}_i^\ast|}{|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_i|+|\mathcal{T}_i^\ast|},
\qquad
\overline{\mathrm{F1}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{F1}_i.
\label{eq:ate-f1}
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{T}_i^\ast$ is the reference set for report $i$, and $n$ is the number of reports in the evaluation.

\subsubsection{Reasoning Pipeline for Analytical Inference: Technique Identification (ATA)}

Another reasoning-based cognitive task is technique identification. \textbf{CTA-ATA} employs the same reasoning approach as CTA-ATE, but with a different contract: a single ATT\&CK identifier. The retriever constructs four lists without referencing the gold identifier: identifiers already present in a lexical memo of related CTI chunks, procedure-example ranks, technique-definition ranks, and cue-to-identifier anchors. We fuse the four lists with reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009rrf}. An identifier $d$ at rank $r_i(d)$ in list $i$ receives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RRF}(d)=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{k+r_i(d)},
\qquad k{=}60.
\label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}
Rank $r_i(d)$ starts at $1$. The constant $k{=}60$ is the published default of Cormack et al., and we fix it before scoring the evaluation items. A list that omits $d$ adds nothing. The five highest-scoring identifiers are formatted as ATT\&CK cards and passed to the generator. In the main evaluation the generator may return any technique identifier. A second evaluation restricts the answer to the five retrieved identifiers.

\subsubsection{Reasoning Pipeline for Analytical Inference: Actor Attribution (TAA)}
The \textbf{CTA-TAA} names one actor. In this model, we build three lists without gold labels: an evidence-query ranking over intrusion-set passages, five free-form hypotheses from the anonymized report, and five catalogue-constrained hypotheses from the evidence excerpts. Each rank is rescaled to the unit interval and the rescaled scores are added with equal weight. The rescaling step is min--max normalization \citeyearpar{lee1997ir}, and the addition step is CombSUM \citeyearpar{fox1994combination}. The two papers define the rules, which explains the early dates. Reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009rrf}, used for technique identifiers, is the later member of the same family: combine several rankings with weights fixed before the evaluation labels are read.
\begin{align}
s_i(a)&=1-\frac{r_i(a)-1}{|L_i|-1},\label{eq:taa-norm}\\
\mathrm{CombSUM}(a)&=\sum_{i}s_i(a),\label{eq:taa-combsum}\\
R_{\mathrm{TAA}}(d)&=\mathrm{top}\text{-}3_a\,\mathrm{CombSUM}(a),\label{eq:taa3}\\
A_{\mathrm{TAA}}(d)&=G_{\mathrm{attr}}\bigl(d,R_{\mathrm{TAA}}(d)\bigr)\label{eq:taa4}\\
&\in\{1,2,3,\mathrm{NO\_MATCH}\}.\nonumber
\end{align}
$L_i$ is source list $i$, $r_i(a)$ is the rank of actor $a$ in that list, and $s_i(a)$ is the rescaled rank. An actor missing from a list scores zero. We keep the three source weights equal, and we fix the weights before reading the evaluation labels. The generator sees the report and the three names and returns one of the three shortlisted names, or abstains. Section~\ref{sec:taa-ata-pipeline} states which shortlist the completed generation run used.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Benchmark and cognitive roles}\label{sec:benchmark}

Following the design of the six specialist pipelines, we evaluate the framework on the corresponding CTI benchmark tasks. The main evaluation uses five CTIBench datasets \citep{alam2024ctibench}: multiple-choice questions (MCQ, $n{=}2{,}500$), root-cause mapping (RCM, $n{=}1{,}000$), vulnerability severity prediction (VSP, $n{=}1{,}000$), attack technique extraction (ATE, $n{=}60$), and threat-actor attribution (TAA, $n{=}50$). We further evaluate technique attribution (ATA, $n{=}160$) on CTIConnect and use its 290 matched items for external root-cause mapping evaluation \citep{cheng2026cticonnect}. Each count corresponds to the full item count of the published file and serves as the denominator for answer accuracy. All systems are evaluated on the same item identifiers within each dataset.

We first examine whether the cascade selects the specialist specified in each instruction. It agrees with the named specialist on 2,498 of 2,500 MCQ items, 992 of 1,000 RCM items, all 1,000 VSP items, all 60 ATE items, all 50 TAA items, and 142 of 160 ATA items, yielding 4,742 agreements across 4,770 instructions (Table~\ref{tab:route-accuracy}). The remaining two MCQ items, eight RCM items, and 18 ATA items are routed to a different specialist. The two MCQ items and eight RCM items remain in their original answer-accuracy denominators, and their answers are scored from the specialist actually selected by the cascade. VSP, ATE, and TAA match the named specialist on every item. The 18 ATA misses are a router-only check: they do not change the ATA answer denominator, which still scores the designated ATA specialist.

We then assess the answers produced by each specialist using the metric defined for its task. MCQ accuracy measures the fraction of items for which the parsed answer letter matches the reference; unparsed responses and responses without a letter count as incorrect. RCM accuracy requires an exact match between the predicted and reference CWE identifiers. For VSP, we report the mean absolute deviation (MAD) of CVSS base scores. When the predicted vector parses, the item error is the absolute difference between the predicted and reference base scores; malformed vectors and failed calls receive an error of 10. ATE is evaluated using the mean report-level F1 between the predicted and reference technique-identifier sets, after mapping sub-techniques to their parent techniques. ATA uses exact-match accuracy on technique identifiers, while TAA uses alias matching on actor names.

For ATA and TAA, we additionally evaluate retrieval performance before generation using Recall@$k$, which measures the fraction of items whose reference technique identifier or actor appears among the top-$k$ retrieved candidates. This allows us to examine candidate retrieval separately from the generator's final prediction. End-to-end accuracy measures whether the generated answer matches the reference, whereas route accuracy measures whether the request reaches the specialist specified in its instruction. These metrics capture different stages of the framework: selecting the intended specialist does not necessarily guarantee a correct answer.

\subsection{Knowledge infrastructure}\label{sec:kb}
CTA-RAG loads six local stores built from public CTI sources, one store for each specialist:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
\item \texttt{memorization\_vdb}: web CTI and ATT\&CK-linked pages, dense FAISS, used by MCQ.
\item General CTI knowledge base: narrative CTI chunks for RCM; the optional MITRE CWE FAISS arm is off in the defended configuration.
\item \texttt{problem\_solving\_vdb}: sanitized NVD/CVE descriptions for VSP analogues.
\item ATT\&CK domain index: technique text for ATE.
\item ATA hybrid pool: MITRE STIX \texttt{enterprise-attack.json} (technique definitions, procedure examples, \texttt{uses} relations) plus lexical memo chunks from the memorization corpus.
\item TAA profiles: MITRE intrusion-set records (canonical name, aliases, malware, tools, campaigns).
\end{itemize}

Query-time sanitisation strips answer-bearing CVE metadata from VSP neighbours. The stores are built from public CTI text, and the benchmarks quote the same public sources. A retrieved passage can therefore repeat wording that also appears in the test item. In that case a retrieval score can look stronger than on text absent from the index. The numbers in this paper include that possibility. Removing benchmark sentences from the index before retrieval is the check that would separate repeated source text from evidence the model still has to use.

\subsection{Controlled baselines}\label{sec:baselines}
We implemented six comparison systems in this study, and we ran CTA-RAG beside the six systems. Every system sees the same items and the same generator, GPT-4-turbo, so a difference in score comes from retrieval, prompting, and parsing. Table~\ref{tab:main} uses the names below.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Closed Book.} The model sees the item and the task prompt. We retrieve no passages.
\item \textbf{Unified RAG.} We follow the original retrieve-then-generate pattern \citep{lewis2020rag}: one dense top-$k$ search over one shared corpus, then one generation call, for every task.
\item \textbf{Self-Critique RAG.} Following Self-RAG \citep{asai2023selfrag}, the model may retrieve, critique the passages, and skip empty or low-value context before answering.
\item \textbf{Entity-Guided Multi-Query RAG.} Following GraphRAG \citep{edge2024graphrag}, named entities in the item create extra queries, and we append the entity passages to one shared prompt. We implemented the GraphRAG idea on our index so the comparison uses our corpus and our generator. Entity-Guided RAG keeps one index and one parser for every task. CTA-RAG selects a specialist, searches that specialist's index, and applies that specialist's prompt and parser.
\item \textbf{Adaptive-RAG.} Following Jeong et al.\ \citep{jeong2024adaptive}, a complexity estimate selects retrieval depth: none, one hop, or iterative retrieval, and then generation.
\item \textbf{Dual-Query RAG.} Following TAdaRAG \citep{zhang2026tadarag}, a second, task-shaped query is issued beside the original item.
\item \textbf{CTA-RAG.} The specialist in Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config}, with its own index, prompt, and parser.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is held fixed}\label{sec:integrity}
Every system answers the same items with GPT-4-turbo. CTA-RAG uses the specialist index, prompt, and parser in Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config}. Each comparison system uses the retrieval procedure in Section~\ref{sec:baselines} and the prompt we wrote for that task. A difference in Table~\ref{tab:main} is a difference among the pipelines.

Each specialist has its own decoding temperature, listed in Table~\ref{tab:pipe-config}. Temperatures stay at or near zero so that a difference between systems reflects the pipeline. Technique extraction and technique attribution use temperature $0$ because each answer is an identifier string. The comparison systems use GPT-4-turbo at temperature $0$, with $top\_p{=}1$ and an 800-token budget. After decoding, the weakness-mapping parser either rejects the string or accepts a CWE. Section~\ref{sec:hallucination} scores the accepted-but-wrong case as a hallucination.

For TAA and ATA the metrics in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} split retrieval from the final answer. Recall@$k$ asks whether the reference actor or technique identifier is in the retrieved list. End-to-end accuracy scores the parsed answer. On ATA, CTA-RAG and Entity-Guided RAG may both return any technique identifier, including one absent from the five cards. A second CTA-RAG run restricts the answer to the five retrieved identifiers. On TAA, every reported system chooses among the three actors in its own shortlist, or abstains. An invalid categorical output counts as incorrect.

\subsection{How the auxiliary lists are built}\label{sec:taa-ata-pipeline}
We keep the reference actor and the reference technique identifier out of the query, the list, and the prompt.

For TAA we fuse the three lists in Section~\ref{sec:pipelines} with equal-weight CombSUM (Eq.~\eqref{eq:taa-combsum}). The generation run asks the model to choose among the three names on each system's evidence-query shortlist.

For ATA we fuse a lexical memo, procedure ranks ($k{=}8$), definition ranks ($k{=}8$), and cue anchors with reciprocal rank fusion, constant $k{=}60$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rrf}). The generator may return any technique identifier. A second run accepts only one of the five retrieved identifiers.

\paragraph{Multiple-choice questions.}
On MCQ ($n{=}2{,}500$), Adaptive-RAG reaches 75.48\% and CTA-RAG reaches 74.84\%, a difference of 16 questions. Closed Book is 71.00\%, and every retrieval system sits above that line. The paired interval of CTA-RAG against Adaptive-RAG includes zero after Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:paired-focus}). On this dataset, passages from the shared corpus already supply the fact the letter asks for, and the specialist pipeline adds no further demonstrated gain.

\paragraph{Root-cause mapping.}
On RCM ($n{=}1{,}000$), CTA-RAG is first, at 73.40\%. Unified RAG is 68.70\% and Adaptive-RAG, the next retrieval system, is 69.50\%. That lead is 47 more correct weakness classes than Unified RAG. The RCM specialist inside CTA-RAG searches the narrative knowledge base and must return one primary CWE. A sibling CWE is a legal identifier and still the wrong class, so this specialist leaves the optional CWE-catalogue neighbours out. The Holm-corrected test supports the gain over Unified RAG ($p{=}0.0213$). The 3.90-point gain over Adaptive-RAG has $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.0730$, inside the corrected threshold. CTA-RAG therefore leads the point estimate, and the confirmed gain is against the shared RAG pipeline.

\paragraph{Vulnerability severity prediction.}
On VSP ($n{=}1{,}000$), a lower mean absolute deviation is a closer severity score. CTA-RAG is first, at 1.0990. Unified RAG is 1.2638 and Adaptive-RAG is 1.2170. The VSP specialist inside CTA-RAG reads sanitised historical vulnerability descriptions and returns the eight CVSS base metrics through its own parser. The comparison systems receive the same CVSS instructions, so the lower error comes from the analogue descriptions and from the VSP parser. The reduction of 0.1648 against Unified RAG has $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.0483$, close to 0.05. The reduction of 0.1180 against Adaptive-RAG has $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.3237$. CTA-RAG therefore leads severity scoring, and the confirmed gain is against Unified RAG.

On the 992 items every system completed, CTA-RAG and Adaptive-RAG reach 1.0272 and 1.2073. We report that cut as a sensitivity check. Dropping the failed calls changes the denominator and increases the estimated benefit.

\paragraph{Attack technique extraction.}
On ATE ($n{=}60$), Unified RAG lifts mean F1 from the Closed Book value of 0.4149 to 0.9167. The other shared-retrieval systems fall between 0.9133 and 0.9178. CTA-RAG is highest at 0.9554. With 60 reports the extra 0.0386 over Unified RAG has $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}{=}0.4527$. Every saved ATE response parses, so the Closed Book deficit is missing and wrong technique identifiers. A larger extraction set is what would test the remaining margin again.

Across MCQ, RCM, VSP, and ATE, the gain appears where a specialist inside CTA-RAG must return one constrained decision. The RCM specialist returns one CWE identifier. The VSP specialist returns one CVSS vector. On a factual letter, adaptive retrieval on the shared corpus leads the point estimate. On technique extraction, the ATE specialist's ATT\&CK index accounts for most of the gain over Closed Book. Each specialist keeps its own index, prompt, and parser, so each gain belongs to a specialist inside CTA-RAG. TAA completes RQ1 in Section~\ref{sec:taa-results}: CTA-RAG names the correct actor on 27 of 50 reports.

\paragraph{MCQ and RCM show different retrieval behaviour.}
Unified RAG rescues 149 MCQ answers while damaging 76, resulting in
73 additional correct answers. CTA-RAG produces a larger net MCQ gain,
with 267 rescues and 171 damages. Both systems therefore benefit from
retrieval on MCQ, although the gains coexist with regressions on individual
questions.

The RCM pattern is substantially different. Unified RAG produces 38 rescues
and 40 damages, resulting in a small net loss of two answers. Retrieval
changes the prediction on many items but provides almost no aggregate
benefit because helpful and harmful changes are approximately balanced.
CTA-RAG produces 125 rescues and 80 damages, yielding 45 additional correct
answers over Closed Book.

A direct comparison between CTA-RAG and Unified RAG shows that CTA-RAG
repairs 124 Unified RAG errors while losing 77 Unified RAG correct answers.
The resulting net improvement of 47 items corresponds to the 4.70-point
accuracy difference reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}. CTA-RAG still damages
77 answers that Unified RAG had right. The gain is a better balance between
the repairs and the damages.

\paragraph{VSP and ATE benefit more consistently.}
For VSP, Unified RAG reduces error on 391 items and increases it on 341.
CTA-RAG improves 500 items and worsens 267. The CTA-RAG benefit is spread across many predictions, and the size of each item-level change also enters the mean.

Retrieval is most consistent on ATE, always measured against Closed Book on the same report. Unified RAG raises per-item F1 on 54 of 60 items and lowers it on four. CTA-RAG raises F1 on 58 items and matches Closed Book on the other two, so CTA-RAG records zero losses against Closed Book on this 60-report sample. The ATT\&CK index and the required technique-identifier line are what move the other 58. Closed Book starts at 0.4149 because many reference techniques are missing from the parametric answer. A larger ATE set could still show item-level losses.

The count of improved and worsened items is a separate reading from the
metric difference. VSP errors and ATE F1 changes vary in magnitude,
so the average score also depends on how large each item-level change is.

\paragraph{Illustrative prediction changes.}
The paired transitions illustrate both effects of retrieval. On the first
ATE item, describing 3PARA RAT, Closed Book predicts the four reference
techniques together with \texttt{T1027} and \texttt{T1552} (F1 $=0.80$),
whereas Unified RAG and CTA-RAG recover the reference set exactly
(F1 $=1.00$). Conversely, on RCM item 6, Closed Book correctly predicts
\texttt{CWE-269}, while Unified RAG predicts \texttt{CWE-78} and CTA-RAG
predicts \texttt{CWE-732}. Thus, retrieval can both remove unsupported
predictions and introduce plausible but incorrect identifiers.

Overall, retrieval is most consistently beneficial for ATE and substantially
reduces VSP error, while its effect on exact CWE mapping is less uniform.
The paired analysis measures the full pipeline: retrieval, prompting, routing, and output handling. An item-level change belongs to that pipeline as a whole.

\subsection{Hallucination versus schema failure}
\label{sec:hallucination}

A hallucination here is a CWE the parser accepts that names the wrong weakness \citep{maynez2020faithfulness,ji2023survey}. A schema failure is a string the parser rejects. Weakness mapping is where that split is measurable: the output is one catalogue identifier, so a legal CWE can look successful and still be wrong. Accuracy, MAD, technique F1, and exact identifier match already score a wrong answer as wrong on the other tasks. Table~\ref{tab:hallucination-rcm} is the hallucination count for the external set.

Of the 290 CTIConnect items, CTA-RAG is correct on 149, hallucinates on 134, and fails to parse on 7 (Table~\ref{tab:hallucination-rcm}). The 97.59\% parse rate is the share that produced a CWE string, which is why it sits above the 149 correct mappings. On CTIBench RCM the same definition gives CTA-RAG 734 correct, 196 hallucinations, and 70 schema failures out of 1,000.

\section{Operational Implications}\label{sec:impact}
%%==============================================================================

The strongest practical implication is to select a pipeline by the decision
it must support. Shared retrieval is competitive for factual multiple-choice
questions and supplies substantial benefit for technique extraction; weakness
mapping and severity scoring benefit from closer alignment between evidence
and the required answer. The paired analysis also shows that improvements
are accompanied by regressions on other items. An analyst-facing deployment
should therefore evaluate consequential errors, escalation thresholds, and
review burden. A higher average score is a separate question from safer triage.
Measuring consequential errors and review burden requires a workflow study beyond the benchmark.

Structured outputs make some failures observable and support rejection,
retry, or human review before downstream use. Semantic correctness and injection resistance stay open: an incorrect or attacker-influenced
answer can still be a valid CWE or CVSS vector. A practical interface should
request the intended output explicitly, clarify ambiguous requests, and admit
abstention outside the specialist inventory. Complete timing and token
instrumentation is also needed before claiming efficiency benefits. Explicit
requests, clarification, and abstention complement specialisation, and shared
RAG systems can adopt the same controls.

\paragraph{Maintenance cost of specialisation.}
CTA-RAG shares model-client utilities, embedding interfaces, vector-store
components, and evaluation code, but maintains task-specific retrieval
configurations, prompts, and output handling. Each specialist therefore
requires separate regression coverage, and source updates must be checked
against each consuming pipeline. Adding a task expands this
configuration and testing surface beyond extending a shared prompt. Developer
person-hours fall outside our measurements, so the paper makes no quantified
maintenance claim.

%%==============================================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%%==============================================================================

\paragraph{Benchmark coverage and generalisation.}
RQ1 covers five CTIBench tasks. MCQ, RCM, VSP, and ATE share Table~\ref{tab:main}. TAA uses actor match and is reported with that metric. ATA keeps a separate subsection because the CTIConnect items use a single-identifier contract. The external result covers weakness mapping on CTIConnect. ATE has 60 items, and the extra specialist margin on ATE falls inside the Holm-corrected uncertainty. The paired tests quantify item-sampling uncertainty for the saved predictions and treat evaluation items as independent. Shared reports or related CVEs could make the intervals too narrow. Route accuracy is the share of instructions sent to the named specialist (Table~\ref{tab:route-accuracy}). Answer accuracy on the four CTIBench tasks is the score reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}. ATA route misses were not re-generated on the wrong specialist.
The evaluated datasets, prompts, and routing cues are English, and retrieval
is evaluated in an English-language setting. Dependence on English schema
terms and possible changes in embedding behaviour limit multilingual claims.
Translated or native-language CTI benchmarks and language-aware routing and
retrieval are needed to assess that scope.

\paragraph{Baseline fidelity and causal attribution.}
Self-Critique RAG, Entity-Guided Multi-Query RAG, Adaptive-RAG, and
Dual-Query RAG denote the mechanisms we implemented from each cited paper,
and each upstream architecture has further components. CTA-RAG differs in
prompts, indexes, and pipeline behaviour, so a paired gain combines the
effect of specialisation with the effect of retrieval. The task-level
mechanisms discussed above are interpretations that the design and the
diagnostics support, and a mediation analysis would test each mechanism
directly. The statistical analysis is retrospective. We test all 24 primary
contrasts and apply one joint Holm correction to limit selective reporting.
The 50-item TAA set was used both to design CombSUM and to score its recall, so that recall figure is an in-sample diagnostic. End-to-end TAA scores the generator's choice among three retrieved names. ATA candidate recall is gold-in-list at five cards. Free generation can still name the gold identifier when that list misses it. Restricted generation can return only a retrieved identifier. Entity-Guided RAG at 79/160 and CTA-RAG at 78/160 are aggregate free-generation scores. Item-level pairs between the two runs were outside the audited artefact. Twelve free-generation errors still pick a wrong identifier from a list that already contained the gold identifier.
The four cognitive modes are an operational decomposition by evidence,
reasoning, and output requirements. Other groupings by task identity,
knowledge source, output schema, or retrieval complexity remain untested, so
the evidence supports the cognitive grouping as one workable design. The
controlled shared-corpus comparison uses dense retrieval, and hybrid
BM25--dense retrieval is a separate ablation for future work.

\paragraph{Operational reliability.}
Full-attempt VSP scoring uses an explicit maximum-error penalty for eight
API failures. Another convention for the eight failures can change the effect size.
The common-success subset is a sensitivity check. The primary denominator keeps every item. Invalid RCM outputs remain a substantial weakness
of CTA-RAG. The released logs time different boundaries: CTA-RAG specialist-call medians are 6.11\,s on MCQ, 4.88\,s on RCM, 2.31\,s on VSP, and 10.74\,s on ATE, while the peer logs time the final generation call. Token counts are incomplete, so the medians are an audit of the logs. A latency ranking needs one timer, one repair step, and complete token counts on every system. We recompute the statistics from the saved item-level predictions.

\paragraph{Corpus overlap and security.}
Query-time sanitisation removes selected leakage signals. Independence
between the retrieval corpora and the evaluation items, including the
external dataset, needs index-level exclusion and overlap audits. Poisoning
and prompt-injection attacks remain outside this evaluation. Isolated
task-scoped indexes may limit document exposure, and a measured adversarial
test is required before either compartmentalisation or schema validation
counts as a defence.
The evaluation also assumes sufficiently reliable indexed sources. Temporal
conflict resolution, source-authority weighting, and cross-source
contradiction detection lie outside the current pipelines. Task scoping
leaves stale or erroneous information inside an allowed source unchanged,
and saved retrieval provenance is incomplete. Deployment requires source provenance,
snapshot versioning, update monitoring, and abstention or human review when
authoritative sources disagree.

%%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%%==============================================================================

We built CTA-RAG so that a router selects the CTI artefact before retrieval and one specialist then answers, with its own index, prompt, and parser. We compared that pipeline with six retrieval systems on the same items and the same generator, GPT-4-turbo, under one full-attempt protocol and the official metric for each dataset.

On the five CTIBench tasks in RQ1, CTA-RAG leads root-cause mapping at 73.40\% and vulnerability severity at a mean absolute deviation of 1.0990. Holm-corrected tests support both gains over Unified RAG. Adaptive-RAG leads multiple choice at 75.48\%, and CTA-RAG scores 74.84\%. Every retrieval system lifts technique extraction well above the Closed Book F1 of 0.4149, and CTA-RAG reaches 0.9554. After Holm correction that extra extraction margin sits inside the uncertainty of the 60-report sample. On threat-actor attribution, CTA-RAG names the correct actor on 27 of 50 reports. The router sends 4,742 of 4,770 instructions to the named specialist (Table~\ref{tab:route-accuracy}). That figure is route accuracy. Answer accuracy is a separate score: a correct route can still be a wrong answer, and the answer scores are the ones above.

The practical implication is to choose the pipeline by the artefact. The RCM and VSP specialists inside CTA-RAG move the score when the answer is one CWE identifier or one CVSS vector. Shared or adaptive retrieval is enough for a factual letter. Adding an ATT\&CK index accounts for most of the extraction gain over a no-retrieval answer. Retrieval can also overturn a Closed Book answer that was already right, so a higher average still leaves a set of newly wrong items for review.

Threat-actor attribution on 50 reports and technique attribution on 160 CTIConnect items each use a specialist the dataset already names. We report candidate recall beside the end-to-end decision. On an external set of 290 weakness-mapping items, CTA-RAG is correct on 149, with 7 schema failures and 134 accepted CWEs that name the wrong weakness.

A later study can enlarge the extraction set, test the router on paraphrased requests, and remove benchmark sentences from the index before retrieval. A deployment still needs source provenance, snapshot versioning, and human review when authoritative sources disagree. The evidence in this paper covers the benign benchmark items and the contracts above.
